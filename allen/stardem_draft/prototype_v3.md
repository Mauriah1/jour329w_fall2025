# Race & Diversity on Maryland's Eastern Shore: A Reporter's Guide

**How Coverage Has Evolved and What It Reveals About This Community**

---

## Opening: A Region at a Crossroads

Maryland's Eastern Shore sits at a fascinating intersection of American history—the birthplace of Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman, a region shaped by slavery, civil rights struggles, and waves of immigration. Yet today's coverage reveals a community grappling with profound questions: Who belongs here? Whose stories matter? And how do we honor the past while building an inclusive future?

Since late 2023, reporting has tracked an increasingly polarized landscape. Immigration enforcement intensified dramatically under the Trump administration's second term, with ICE arrests surging 290% in Maryland by mid-2025. The Pride festival in Easton survived council opposition but faced renewed scrutiny. Diversity statements—once routine—became battlegrounds. Meanwhile, historians uncovered the names of over 550 Black Civil War soldiers from Talbot County alone, and Native American communities demanded recognition of their continuing presence.

What emerges is a story of competing visions: between those expanding recognition of marginalized communities and those resisting what they see as political overreach. The coverage reveals not just policy debates but intimate human stories—a pastor detained after 24 years, families hiding during ICE raids, descendants discovering ancestors who fought for freedom.

---

## The Major Story Arcs: Chronological Shifts

### **2023-Early 2024: Foundations and Recognition**
Coverage begins with quieter cultural work—Black History Month planning, discovery of rare Harriet Tubman photographs, commemorations of indigenous heritage. The tone is largely celebratory, focused on preservation and education. Hispanic Heritage Month launches with festivals; Pride events proceed without major incident. Martin Luther King Jr. Day observances honor his legacy while younger voices, like his granddaughter Yolanda Renee King, call for continued action.

The first hints of political tension appear in January 2024 when Easton council members Frank Gunsallus and David Montgomery vote against the Delmarva Pride Festival permit (ultimately approved 3-2), signaling coming battles.

### **Spring-Summer 2024: Federalsburg's Historic Victory**
The landmark moment: Federalsburg's April 2024 settlement after a federal lawsuit under the Voting Rights Act. For 200 years, the town's at-large election system prevented Black residents from holding office despite comprising 43% of the population. The settlement requires a formal apology, commemorative plaque, renaming Brooklyn Avenue, and establishing Sept. 26 as Voting Rights Day—recognizing the 2023 election of the town's first two Black council members, Darlene Hammond and Brandy James.

This represents the Eastern Shore's most significant voting rights victory in generations, cited as a national model for confronting structural racism. Yet it's also controversial, with some residents viewing it as outside interference.

### **Fall 2024-Winter 2025: The DEI Backlash**
As Trump returns to office in January 2025, the political climate shifts sharply. His administration dismantles federal DEI programs, and the ripple effects reach the Shore. By February, Talbot County Council member Dave Stepp proposes repealing the county's diversity statement—adopted unanimously in 2020—citing concerns about federal funding compliance.

The proposal draws fierce opposition. Over 100 residents pack meetings, Pride Center leaders speak of feeling targeted, and council members Lynn Mielke, Pete Lesher, and Keasha Haythe oppose it (Haythe being the council's only Black member). The measure is tabled but resurfaces in March, never formally voted down but effectively dead. Similar controversies roil other jurisdictions.

The National Park Service briefly removes Harriet Tubman content from its Underground Railroad webpage (later restored after outcry), symbolizing broader efforts to downplay racial history.

### **Spring-Summer 2025: Immigration Crackdown**
The most dramatic shift: immigration enforcement intensifies dramatically. On July 21, 2025, ICE agents arrest Pastor Daniel Fuentes Espinal in Easton—a Honduran immigrant who overstayed his visa by 24 years but has no criminal record, a wife, three children, and has led Iglesia del Nazareno Jesús Te Ama for a decade.

His detention sparks protests drawing 150+ people. Legal fees mount (a GoFundMe raises over $26,000). The arrest isn't isolated: in early August, the small border town of Marydel becomes a "ghost town" after multiple ICE operations. Hispanic residents stop going to work; businesses empty. Fifty people march in protest. By mid-2025, the Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center's emergency fund for immigrants—usually kept at $10,000—is severely depleted by the arrest wave.

The crackdown disproportionately affects those without criminal records (federal data shows 41,495 of 57,861 detained have no convictions), contradicting Trump's "worst of the worst" rhetoric.

### **Present (Mid-2025): Competing Narratives**
Coverage reveals two parallel Eastern Shores:

**One Shore** celebrates heritage: Frederick Douglass Day draws hundreds in September 2025; a new exhibit honors 600+ Black Civil War soldiers from Talbot County; Gov. Wes Moore designates 419 "Just Communities" (including Cambridge) to address redlining; Harriet Tubman is posthumously commissioned as brigadier general; the Nause-Waiwash Band unveils a Choptank reservation marker.

**The other Shore** reflects retrenchment: diversity statements under attack; immigration enforcement creating fear; Pride festivals facing ongoing skepticism; debates over whether Columbus Day or Indigenous Peoples' Day better represents American values.

## Key People, Families & Institutions: Who Drives the Story

### **Political Leaders**

**Gov. Wes Moore**: The first Black governor's most significant moves—commissioning Tubman as general, designating Just Communities, announcing $400 million for historically marginalized areas at Cambridge's Bethel AME on Juneteenth 2025. His administration represents the state-level counterweight to federal rollbacks.

**Easton Mayor Megan Cook**: Provides consistent support for Pride events despite council opposition. Speaks openly about being parent to LGBTQ+ son. Represents new generation of Shore leadership.

**Cambridge Mayor Lajan Cephas**: Part of the politically prominent Cephas family (brother Sputty is council president; cousin Charles is Hurlock mayor; cousin Brandy James is Federalsburg council member). The family traces roots to enslaved ancestors Moses and Josiah Cephas, who after the Civil War built the largest Black-owned farm in Dorchester County. Lajan sees governance as service, not primarily politics.

**Easton Council President Frank Gunsallus & Member David Montgomery**: The story's recurring antagonists to progressive causes. Both voted against Pride permit; Gunsallus called the Pride flag "antithetical to America" and "socialist"; Montgomery opposed diversity statement retention. They represent traditionalist resistance to what they see as identity politics.

**Dave Stepp (Talbot County Council)**: Sponsored the resolution to repeal diversity statement, citing federal funding concerns but facing accusations of capitulating to Trumpism.

**Darlene Hammond & Brandy James (Federalsburg)**: Historic firsts—Federalsburg's first Black council members after 200 years. Hammond teared up at the commemorative plaque unveiling; James emphasizes politics as means, not end, to community service.

### **Activists & Advocates**

**Carl Snowden**: Convener of the Caucus of African American Leaders, Maryland's statewide civil rights coalition. Drives major initiatives including the push to rename the Malkus Bridge for Gloria Richardson (not Frederick Malkus, who opposed civil rights legislation). Uses vivid metaphors: "Be like hornets—when you touch one, you touch all." Has been involved in voting rights cases for 30+ years; calls Federalsburg settlement unprecedented nationally for a municipality apologizing.

**Willie G. Woods**: President, Caroline County NAACP. Key to Federalsburg case and celebrating the victory.

**Victoria Gomez Lozano**: Deputy Director, Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center; appointed to Governor's Commission on Hispanic Affairs. A Mexican immigrant who arrived 16 years ago, she's become the face of Latino advocacy on the Shore, coordinating festivals, providing immigration assistance, and combating misinformation as ICE activity surges.

**Tina Jones**: Chair, Delmarva Pride Center Board. A transgender woman who speaks openly about lives being "at stake" when DEI is dismissed. Leads response to Pride festival opposition and expanding LGBTQ+ services across the Shore with new chapter model.

**Kyle O'Donnell**: Delmarva Pride co-founder and treasurer. Emphasizes family-friendly nature of Pride events (asking performers to avoid profanity); sees 2025 as progress after 2024's narrow permit approval.

### **Religious Leaders**

**Rev. Devan Martin** & **Rev. Yvonne Wallace Penn**: Speakers at commemorative events who frame civil rights as ongoing spiritual work.

**Pastor Daniel Fuentes Espinal**: The story's human center for immigration issues. Honduan-born, 24 years in Easton, pastor at Iglesia del Nazareno Jesús Te Ama since 2015. His July 2025 ICE detention becomes rallying point for those opposing Trump immigration policy. Has three children with wife; trying to obtain citizenship legally for years.

**Rev. Elmer Davis (Easton Council)**: Provides moral voice on council: "We should learn to love everybody." Counterbalance to Gunsallus/Montgomery.

### **Historians & Educators**

**Dr. Clara Small**: Retired Salisbury University professor, expert on Black watermen and Eastern Shore African American history. Delivers moving lecture for Talbot's U.S. Colored Troops exhibit. Author of "Compass Points" series.

**Chris Pupke**: Queen Anne's County Historical Society past president, Kennard advisor. Leads effort to document and memorialize USCT soldiers. Discovered that over 435 QA County Black men served.

**Jaelon Terrele Moaney**: Appointed by Moore to Maryland Commission on African American History and Culture. Youngest commissioner, covers Delmarva. Founded Needle's Eye Academy literacy program. Delivers Frederick Douglass speeches; emphasizes regional Black history often overlooked. Descendant of Eastern Shore founding Black families.

**Alex Green**: Harriet Tubman Tours operator who discovered rare 1908 Tubman photo. Emphasizes Tubman's life as transcending tragedy—"shows an incredible woman in a difficult time."

**Anthony Cohen**: Menare Foundation director, historian who walked Underground Railroad route from Maryland to Canada in 1996 (planning 30th anniversary repeat). Creates audio guides including Rooted Wisdom Walks at Adkins Arboretum.

**Julie Schablitsky**: Maryland DOT cultural resources director. Led archeological team discovering Harriet Tubman's likely birthplace—her father Ben Ross's cabin—yielding artifacts including eagle-motif pipe (symbol of resistance?).

### **Families & Descendants**

**The Cephas Family**: As noted, politically prominent across multiple Shore towns. Trace ancestry to enslaved Moses Cephas and post-Civil War landowners. Charles Cephas (75, Hurlock mayor) has 14 children, 49+ grandchildren. Family values center on faith, service, education. "It's not about me," is recurring theme—service is collective, not individual.

**Kenneth Morris Jr.**: Frederick Douglass's great-great-great-grandson. At historical marker unveiling, emphasizes Anna Murray (Douglass's first wife) as "equal partner" whose sacrifice enabled his escape.

**Harriette Lowery**: Founding Frederick Douglass Honor Society member, didn't know Douglass was from Talbot until moving back as adult. Now ensures his legacy reaches younger generations. Has 10+ relatives in Unionville cemetery (Black Civil War soldiers).

**Adrian (Marydel)**: 17-year-old who had four family members—dad, brother, uncle, cousin—detained by ICE in one day (August 2025). Represents generation seeing immigration enforcement split families.

### **Indigenous Leadership**

**Chief Donna "Wolf Mother" Abbott**: Nause-Waiwash Band of Indians' first female chief (elected 2014). Initially reluctant—"it's not about me"—but recognized duty to preserve history being lost. Fights stereotyping (she has blue-gray eyes, passes as white). Leading effort to restore tribal identity, educate public, partner with conservation groups. Says July 4 is "complicated" for Native Americans—colonists learned freedom from Indigenous peoples already here.

## Geographical Patterns: Mapping the Coverage

### **Talbot County (Easton epicenter)**
Dominates coverage—the county seat becomes the main stage for Pride controversies, DEI debates, and heritage celebrations. Easton is where Douglass statue stands, where Pride festival happens, where ICE arrests spark largest protests. Also where Gunsallus/Montgomery opposition is based. The courthouse lawn is site of major commemorations.

**Why it matters**: Easton's identity as "cultured" Eastern Shore hub is tested by these conflicts. Is it inclusive or exclusive? Wealthy but welcoming, or defensive of traditional hierarchies?

**Notable**: Talbot contributed more Black Union soldiers (600-700) than any Maryland county—a fact only recently fully documented.

### **Dorchester County (Cambridge)**
Second-most prominent. Cambridge is civil rights ground zero—Gloria Richardson led the Cambridge Movement here in the 1960s; Bethel AME Church on Pine Street was headquarters. Gov. Moore chose this location for Juneteenth 2025 Just Communities announcement, understanding symbolic power.

Also Harriet Tubman's birthplace (Church Creek area). The county contains Tubman museum, archeological sites, byways. Increasingly, reporters frame Dorchester as place where past and present collide—civil rights history meets current struggles over economic equity.

**Underrepresented angle**: How Pine Street/"Black Wall Street" remains scarred from 1967 fire (after H. Rap Brown's speech). Just Communities designation aims to address this, but coverage could explore more deeply what decades of disinvestment look like.

### **Caroline County (Denton, Federalsburg, Marydel)**
Federalsburg's voting rights victory is historic, but Marydel emerges as immigration story's emotional center. This tiny border town (176 population, 65% Hispanic in Maryland side) becomes "ghost town" after August 2025 ICE operations. Young Hispanic protesters march through mobile home parks. The demographic transformation of Caroline's northern corridor (Marydel, Templeville, Henderson all 65-70% Hispanic) is dramatic and under-covered.

**Coverage gap**: What is the economic role of this population? How do white longtime residents view the change? Are there success stories of integration?

### **Queen Anne's County (Centreville)**
Kennard African American Cultural Heritage Center is key institution. QA County contributed 435+ USCT soldiers—now memorialized with plaque. Centreville's Bethel AME hosts exhibits. But QA remains somewhat underrepresented given its significant Black population and history.

### **Kent County (Chestertown)**
Strong institutional coverage through Washington College's Starr Center, Sumner Hall (GAR Post #25), and annual Legacy Day. Chestertown hosts MLK breakfast, Juneteenth "Heroes of the Chesapeake," and Freedom Riders documentary premiere. The town seems more comfortable celebrating history than some Shore communities, but coverage could explore whether that translates to current equity.

### **Underrepresented Communities**
**Somerset County**: Barely mentioned despite significant Black and Latino populations. Crisfield, Princess Anne—where are their stories?

**Worcester County**: Ocean City's diversity issues rarely covered in this corpus.

**Wicomico County (Salisbury)**: Receives immigrants at high rates (more per capita than LA County post-2020) but mostly covered through ICE enforcement lens, not community integration stories.

**Rural Communities**: Small towns between the county seats—places like St. Michaels, Oxford, Trappe—appear sporadically. Are they havens of quiet integration or pockets of resistance?

## The Chronological Arc: Cause, Effect, Escalation

**Stage 1 (2023-Early 2024): Cultural Preservation**
Emphasis on education, commemoration, recovery of lost history. Tubman photographs surface, Civil War soldiers named, Native American markers unveiled. Political tension is low-level (Pride permit vote a warning sign).

**Stage 2 (Spring 2024): Structural Victory**
Federalsburg settlement represents breakthrough—structural racism acknowledged, legally remedied, officially apologized for. Creates model and momentum. Moore administration active in promoting equity initiatives.

**Stage 3 (Fall 2024-Winter 2025): Backlash Begins**
Trump's return catalyzes pushback. DEI suddenly framed as federal compliance issue, not moral imperative. Language shifts from "inclusion" to "divisiveness." Those who felt "left behind" by equity initiatives find political permission to resist.

**Stage 4 (Spring-Summer 2025): Enforcement & Fear**
Immigration crackdown creates climate of fear in Latino communities. Pastor's arrest crystallizes debate: is this about rule of law or cruelty? Marydel's transformation into ghost town shows real-world impact. Multicultural Resource Center overwhelmed.

Meanwhile, Pride festivals continue but under cloud. Diversity statements remain under attack. Indigenous and Black communities press for recognition but face questions about "rewriting history."

**Stage 5 (Present): Competing Futures**
Two narratives exist simultaneously: 
- One of progress (exhibits, commemorations, Just Communities, expanded recognition)
- One of retrenchment (enforcement, backlash, resistance to DEI)

The escalation pattern: Initial cultural work → Political victories → Organized resistance → Enforcement/intimidation → Continued resilience but at higher cost.

**Cause & Effect Example**: Federalsburg's victory (cause) inspires other communities but also triggers resistance to "outside" interference (effect). Trump's immigration rhetoric (cause) leads to ICE surge (effect) which depletes resources and creates fear (effect) which reduces immigrant community visibility (effect) which threatens Shore's agricultural/seafood economies (potential effect—underreported).

**Continuity**: Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman remain touchstones throughout—their stories invoked by all sides. Douglass's "What to the Slave is the Fourth of July?" speech read annually becomes more resonant as political divisions deepen. The past isn't past; it's argument.

## Glossary for New Reporters

**At-Large Elections**: System where all voters in a jurisdiction elect all representatives, regardless of district. Can dilute minority voting power if majority votes as bloc. Federalsburg's at-large system prevented Black representation for 200 years.

**Byway/Harriet Tubman Byway**: Scenic and historic route with audio tours, maps, landmarks tracing Tubman's journey to freedom. Managed by state, crucial to heritage tourism.

**CBP One App**: Mobile application for migrants to schedule asylum appointments at U.S. border. Trump administration ended access.

**DEI (Diversity, Equity & Inclusion)**: Organizational frameworks/policies promoting diverse hiring, equitable opportunity, inclusive culture. Became politically divisive; Trump administration banned in federal government. Critics call it "reverse discrimination"; supporters say it addresses systemic inequities.

**DoCo Pride**: Dorchester County chapter of Delmarva Pride Center.

**Frederick Douglass Honor Society**: Easton-based group that erected Douglass statue (2011), hosts annual Frederick Douglass Day, preserves his legacy. Founded by Harriette Lowery, Walter Black, and others after surprise that many locals didn't know Douglass's Talbot County origins.

**GAR (Grand Army of the Republic)**: Civil War Union veterans organization. Post #25 in Chestertown (Sumner Hall) was chartered 1882 by Black veterans. Named for Senator Charles Sumner, abolitionist.

**Green Card**: Legal permanent resident status. Pastor Espinal didn't have one (overstayed visa); distinction matters for deportation defense.

**Haitian Development Center of Delmarva**: Salisbury-based organization serving Haitian immigrants who arrived under Biden's humanitarian parole program (2023), which Trump ended. Uncertainty about their future status.

**ICE (Immigration and Customs Enforcement)**: Federal agency under Homeland Security that enforces immigration law, conducts arrests and deportations. Increased activity 290% in Maryland under Trump's second term.

**Jim Crow**: State and local laws enforcing racial segregation, primarily in South, roughly 1877-1965. Eastern Shore maintained Jim Crow systems; Cambridge didn't desegregate until 1960s after violent protests.

**Just Communities**: Moore administration designation (2024) identifying 419 Maryland census tracts that experienced historical disinvestment due to redlining, discriminatory zoning, etc. Provides funding priority, pathway to revitalization. Cambridge is one.

**Kennard African American Cultural Heritage Center**: Centreville (QA County) institution preserving Black history. Named for Black school; houses archives, hosts exhibits, Juneteenth events.

**Lynch Law**: Extrajudicial killing by mob, overwhelmingly targeting Black Americans. Isaiah Fountain (1919-1921) faced multiple lynching attempts in Talbot; his case revealed depth of racial violence on Shore.

**Maryland Commission on African American History & Culture**: First state-level ethnic commission in U.S. (est. 1969). Awards $5M annually for heritage preservation. Jaelon Moaney represents Eastern Shore.

**Middle Passage**: Brutal transatlantic crossing of enslaved Africans. "Souls at Sea" ceremony at Chesapeake Bay Maritime Museum honors those who perished. Oxford was documented arrival port 1763-1772 (634 transported, 99 died en route).

**Nause-Waiwash Band of Indians**: Descendants of Nanticoke, Choptank, Pocomoke tribes. About 300 members. Not state-recognized but represented on Maryland Commission on Indian Affairs. Chief Donna Abbott leads preservation efforts. Nause was Nanticoke village (Vienna area); Waiwash was Choptank village (Cambridge area).

**Padrone System**: Exploitative labor arrangement where bosses (padrones) controlled Italian immigrant workers, trapping them in debt. Part of Italian-American discrimination history (relevant to Columbus Day debates).

**Posada Navideña**: Mexican Christmas tradition re-enacting Mary and Joseph seeking shelter. People go door-to-door singing; hosts provide treats. Chesapeake MRC hosted first one in Oxford (2024) to help Latino families maintain cultural connection.

**Redlining**: Discriminatory practice where banks/insurers refuse services to residents of certain areas based on racial composition. Created persistent wealth gaps. "Just Communities" designation addresses legacy.

**Religious Crimes Code**: Federal law (1880s-1930s) banning Native American ceremonies/dancing. Harsh punishments drove practices underground or to July 4 (disguised as patriotic celebration). Explains why many tribal gatherings still occur near Independence Day.

**Reverse Underground Railroad**: Illegal slave trade where bounty hunters (like Patty Cannon) captured escaped/freed Black people to sell them back into slavery. Cephas family members were victims.

**Smudging**: Indigenous cleansing ceremony burning herbs (sage, sweetgrass), using feather to guide smoke. Performed at Choptank Reservation marker unveiling.

**Talbot Boys**: Confederate soldier monument that stood on Talbot Courthouse lawn until 2022 relocation after years of controversy. Symbolizes lingering tensions over Civil War memory.

**TPS (Temporary Protected Status)**: Immigration status allowing people from designated countries facing crisis to live/work in U.S. Trump ended protections for many. Salisbury has large Haitian TPS population.

**Treaty of Cambridge (1963)**: Agreement brokered by Attorney General Robert Kennedy with Gloria Richardson and civil rights activists addressing desegregation, housing, employment. Richardson controversially persuaded followers to boycott public accommodations referendum, arguing rights shouldn't be subject to majority vote.

**Underground Railroad**: Secret network of routes, safe houses helping enslaved people escape to free states/Canada. Harriet Tubman made 13+ missions, freeing 70+ people. Dorchester County was major corridor.

**USCT (United States Colored Troops)**: Black soldiers in Union Army during Civil War, authorized 1863. Nearly 180,000 served; 8,718 from Maryland. Talbot County led state in enlistments (600-700). Often discounted by military brass but proved valor repeatedly. Faced discrimination (paid less, assigned labor duties, rarely commissioned as officers).

**Voting Rights Act of 1965**: Landmark federal law prohibiting racial discrimination in voting. Federalsburg lawsuit filed under Section 2. Recent Supreme Court decisions weakened it, but still powerful tool.

**Watermen**: Chesapeake Bay fishermen/crabbers/oystermen. Historically significant Black watermen community now down to fewer than 20 from hundreds. MPT documentary "Water's Edge: Black Watermen of the Chesapeake" profiles them. Sen. Cardin held roundtable with Black watermen families (Feb 2024) discussing preservation of their legacy and threatened livelihoods.

**White Savior Complex**: Problematic dynamic where white people center themselves in racial justice work rather than supporting Black-led initiatives. Relevant to debates over who tells Eastern Shore stories.

**Zoombombing**: Online harassment where provocateurs crash virtual meetings with offensive content. Newark City Council meeting disrupted May 2024 by racist slurs including n-word during Microsoft Teams session. Led to investigation, policy changes.
