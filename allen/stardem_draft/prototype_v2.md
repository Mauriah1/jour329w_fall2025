# Race & Diversity Beat Guide: Maryland's Eastern Shore (2024–2025)

**Welcome to the Beat**

Picture yourself standing at the edge of the Chesapeake Bay on a crisp autumn morning, watching watermen pull crab pots as they've done for generations. Now imagine trying to tell the story of who those watermen are today, and realizing that the narrative you thought you knew—the one passed down through local lore and Chamber of Commerce brochures—is incomplete. 

Welcome to the race and diversity beat on Maryland's Eastern Shore, where history isn't just history. It's happening right now, in real time, in ways that will surprise you.

This isn't Baltimore. This isn't the D.C. suburbs. The Eastern Shore—stretching across Talbot, Dorchester, Caroline, Queen Anne's, and Kent counties—operates on its own rhythm. It's a place where Frederick Douglass was born enslaved and where Harriet Tubman made her daring escapes. It's also a place where, in 2023, a town called Federalsburg elected its first Black council members in its 200-year history. Where Hispanic families now make up 65% of the population in small towns like Marydel. Where Indigenous people are reclaiming narratives about land that was theirs long before it was anyone else's.

What makes this beat unique is the collision: between deep-rooted traditions and rapid demographic change, between rural conservatism and progressive activism, between those who say "we've always done it this way" and those who say "not anymore." You'll cover Confederate statue removals and Pride festivals, ICE raids and Juneteenth celebrations, debates over diversity statements and fights for voting rights. Sometimes all in the same week.

The stories here aren't abstractions. They're Pastor Daniel Fuentes Espinal being detained by ICE after 24 years in Easton. They're Roberta Butler and Jeff Stevenson Jr. winning a federal lawsuit that changed Federalsburg's election system. They're Victoria Gomez Lozano becoming the first Latina appointed to Maryland's Governor's Commission on Hispanic Affairs while running a resource center from a modest office in Easton. They're Chief Donna Wolf Mother Abbott fighting to preserve Nause-Waiwash history even as rising sea levels threaten to erase ancestral lands.

This guide will help you understand how we got here—and where the story is heading next.

## How the Story Unfolded: A Narrative Timeline (2024–2025)

The year began quietly enough. In January 2024, readers learned that Victoria Gomez Lozano, who'd immigrated from Mexico 16 years earlier, had just been appointed to the Governor's Commission on Hispanic Affairs. "It's very exciting for me," she told a reporter, noting she hoped to represent the rural Eastern Shore and its growing Hispanic population. The Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center, where she worked, had served 3,500 people the previous year—triple what they'd expected when they launched a decade prior.

That same month, columnist Jessica A. Johnson reflected on Martin Luther King Jr.'s legacy through the eyes of his 15-year-old granddaughter, Yolanda Renee King, who'd declared her generation would be "defined by action, not apathy." It was aspirational writing, the kind that feels hopeful in January.

By February, the conversation had shifted. The annual Black History Month programming rolled out across the Shore—lectures at the Kennard African American Cultural Heritage Center in Centreville, discussions about the six Black watermen featured in a Maryland Public Television documentary, events celebrating Frederick Douglass's February 20 birthday. U.S. Senator Ben Cardin visited Chesapeake College to meet with descendants of those Black watermen. "The key is connecting the dots to follow the history of Black Marylanders," Cardin said.

Then came February 7, when Jaelon Terrele Moaney, a 30-something Mid-Shore native who'd attended Talbot County schools, was appointed by Governor Wes Moore to the Maryland Commission on African American History and Culture. Moaney wasn't shy about his mission: "Our country is really trying to reconcile with wounds that haven't fully healed or even... scars that are opening up," he said. He promised to bring the commission to the Shore, to tell stories that had been overlooked for too long.

Spring brought a legal earthquake. On April 1, the settlement in the Federalsburg voting rights case was announced. The town—which had maintained an all-white town council for 200 years despite a 43% Black population—agreed to pay $260,000 in legal fees and, more significantly, to issue a formal apology. "The Town of Federalsburg formally acknowledges responsibility and expresses its deep regret," the statement read. A plaque would be installed. Brooklyn Avenue would be restored (it had been renamed Gerardi Boulevard). September 26 would become Voting Rights Day. The settlement required the town to create a photo display honoring Black contributions, establish a Unity Committee, and host an annual multicultural celebration.

"I think I would first start off with an apology," said Darlene Pitt Hammond, one of the two Black women elected to the council in September 2023, tears in her eyes. "It's a lot to think that this small little town was able to stand for the rights of all people, not some people."

But not everyone celebrated. Within days, on April 8, the Caucus of African American Leaders—led by Baltimore civil rights activist Carl Snowden—called for the renaming of the Frederick C. Malkus Jr. Bridge. Malkus, a longtime state senator instrumental in getting the Choptank River bridge built, had also been a vocal opponent of civil rights legislation in the 1960s. The Baltimore Sun had called him "a consistent opponent of civil rights legislation" in 1966. The caucus wanted the bridge renamed for Gloria Richardson, the Cambridge civil rights leader who'd stood down the National Guard during the city's racial unrest in the 1960s.

The proposal drew immediate pushback. Terry Wheatley, who'd worked for Malkus for 23 years, defended him at a Cambridge council meeting: "If Mr. Snowden doesn't want to cross the Frederick C. Malkus Jr. Bridge, I suggest he stay on the other side." The bridge name remains unchanged, though the debate continues.

April also brought a different kind of controversy. On April 2, three 13-year-old students at Plum Point Middle School in Calvert County were charged with hate crimes after displaying swastikas, making Nazi salutes, and directing offensive comments at a Jewish classmate. The behavior had continued for months. "Maryland was founded on the principle of religious toleration," said State's Attorney Robert Harvey. "It is frankly astonishing that nearly 400 years later some people continue to persecute others based upon their religion."

Then, in early May, came another flashpoint: A University of Delaware student named Jenna Kandeel was arrested and charged with a hate crime after vandalizing a Holocaust memorial display and going on an antisemitic tirade. She admitted to damaging ten flags and spitting on them because she felt "frustrated and triggered" by the Holocaust memorial. Attorney General Kathy Jennings condemned it sharply: "The Holocaust is not ancient history. Eighty years later, the world's Jewish population still has not recovered."

By late May, the Newark City Council found itself dealing with a different kind of hate. During a May 13 council meeting, two people participating virtually suddenly unmuted their microphones and began yelling racial slurs, including the n-word. Councilwoman Dwendolyn Creecy, the city's first Black female council member, later called it "one of the loneliest days here for me." The NAACP demanded swift action and called for an end to virtual participation until the matter was settled.

Summer brought the annual Pride celebrations—and the familiar tensions. The Delmarva Pride Festival in Easton had become a flashpoint since its inception in 2021. In 2024, the Easton Town Council had approved the festival's permit by just 3-2, with Council President Frank Gunsallus and member David Montgomery voting no. Montgomery had expressed concerns about "overtly sexualized performances and behavior." By June 2024, as the festival approached again, both men were doubling down. Gunsallus called the Pride flag "antithetical to America" and suggested town funds shouldn't support it. Montgomery said: "This is about the hijacking of the movement by a transexual ideology."

The June 14 festival went off without incident, drawing hundreds. Mayor Megan Cook brought her son and spoke from the stage: "Pride is more than just a festival. It's a powerful reminder of the strength it takes to live authentically." But the damage from the council's rhetoric lingered. Letters poured into the Star Democrat. Some defended Gunsallus and Montgomery, calling Pride a "socialist endeavor" and questioning why other celebrations didn't face the same scrutiny. Others called the council members out: "What message are you sending to remove a statement of kindness, of inclusion, of civility?"

The diversity statement debate exploded in February 2025. Councilmember Dave Stepp of Talbot County proposed repealing the county's diversity, equity, and inclusion statement from the employee handbook. He cited President Trump's executive order ending DEI programs in federal government and claimed the county needed to protect its federal funding. The resolution sparked outrage. More than 100 residents packed the council chambers on February 25, many holding signs opposing the repeal. Council members Keasha Haythe, Pete Lesher, and Lynn Mielke all indicated opposition. "I feel like I'm diversity, equity and inclusion," said Haythe, the council's only Black member. The vote was tabled, but the controversy revealed deep fractures.

Then came the ICE raids.

In late July 2025, Pastor Daniel Fuentes Espinal was detained by ICE agents in Easton. He'd been in the country for 24 years on an expired tourist visa, had three children, led a local Spanish-speaking church, and had no criminal record beyond the immigration violation. His arrest sparked protests, town council debates, and anguished discussions about what it means to be a community. "They're scared. They don't know what's going to happen," said Alfonso Ortiz, a Marydel store owner, as the town emptied out following reports of ICE operations.

By August, the pressure had intensified. Towns like Marydel—population 176, 65% Hispanic—saw their streets go empty as rumors of ICE raids spread. Businesses suffered. Families sent their children to school terrified that parents wouldn't be there when they got home. A rally in Easton drew 150 people demanding Espinal's return. GoFundMe campaigns raised tens of thousands for legal fees. The Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center's emergency fund, normally kept at $10,000, was depleted.

Easton Police Captain Greg Wright confirmed ICE activity but said the department had no formal agreement with the agency and typically wasn't notified of operations. That didn't stop residents from demanding answers at town council meetings. "What's going to happen to the workforce?" one man asked at a meeting. "They need the value that immigrants provide."

And so the year rolled on—Pride festivals and ICE raids, statue controversies and voting rights victories, hate crimes against Jewish students and celebrations of Black watermen, debates over trans rights in the military and the unveiling of historical markers for Harriet Tubman's birthplace.

What emerges from this timeline isn't a single story but a chorus—sometimes harmonious, often discordant, always revealing.

## Major Themes: The Currents Running Beneath

### **The Weight of History (and Who Gets to Tell It)**

If you cover race and diversity on the Eastern Shore long enough, you realize everything comes back to history. Not dusty, distant history, but history that's still shaping who lives where, who holds power, who gets heard.

Take Federalsburg. For 200 years, the town's election system—at-large voting with staggered terms—effectively prevented Black residents from electing representatives despite making up 43% of the population. The system wasn't an accident. It was designed that way. When the Caucus of African American Leaders, the Caroline County NAACP, and local residents sued in federal court under the Voting Rights Act, they won. The settlement required not just changing the election system but acknowledging the harm: "The Town of Federalsburg formally acknowledges responsibility and expresses its deep regret for actions and inactions contributing to racial discrimination and exclusion of Black residents."

Carl Snowden, the Baltimore civil rights leader who helped organize the lawsuit, called it historic: "What Federalsburg has done is become a model to what can happen throughout Maryland and the rest of the nation. In my more than 30 years of involvement in voting rights cases, I have never before seen a municipality willing to admit its wrongs and apologize for it."

Or consider the ongoing fight over the Malkus Bridge. Frederick C. Malkus Jr., the state senator who secured funding for the bridge spanning the Choptank River, was also a fierce opponent of civil rights legislation. He debated George Wallace at Goucher College in the 1960s, denouncing civil rights laws as un-American. He blamed "outsiders" for racial troubles in Cambridge. When the bridge was named after him—while he was still alive—then-Governor Harry Hughes vetoed the bill. The legislature overrode the veto unanimously.

Now, decades later, the question is whether a bridge crossing between two counties should bear the name of someone who fought to keep Black residents as second-class citizens. The Caucus of African American Leaders wants it renamed for Gloria Richardson, the Cambridge civil rights leader who negotiated the Treaty of Cambridge with Attorney General Robert Kennedy in 1963 after weeks of protests and martial law. Richardson, who died in 2021 at age 99, pushed back when officials tried to put desegregation to a referendum: "It was grossly unfair to leave the constitutional rights of our people to the whim of a popular majority."

The debate is revealing. Supporters of keeping Malkus's name point to his role in infrastructure development. Opponents ask why we honor someone who actively worked against equality. Star Democrat editor Jim Johnson put it plainly: "I suggest June is also an appropriate time to ensure Everett Dirksen is not forgotten as a champion in advancing civil rights in America," referring to the Republican senator who helped pass the 1964 Civil Rights Act. "With all due respect to Juneteenth, I suggest June is also an appropriate time to ensure that Frederick C. Malkus is not forgotten for fighting against civil rights."

The pattern repeats across the region. Confederate statues. Historical markers. Street names. School names. Each becomes a battleground over whose history matters, whose contributions get memorialized, whose pain gets acknowledged.

Meanwhile, actual historians are doing the unglamorous work of documenting the past. The Talbot Historical Society spent months researching U.S. Colored Troops from the county, eventually identifying more than 550 Black men who served in the Union Army during the Civil War. Many were enslaved when they enlisted; military service was their path to freedom. They fought at Petersburg, at Fort Gilmer, at Chaffin's Farm. Some were present when Lee surrendered at Appomattox. After the war, they founded Unionville, a community for formerly enslaved people.

An exhibit opened in May 2025, displaying their names and military records. "To date, we have uncovered the names of over 550 men from Talbot County who served in the Union's United States Colored Troops—and we are still discovering more," the Historical Society announced. Historian Clara Small told attendees: "This exhibit will help to put flesh on those names. The goal is that it's not just a name anymore, but a human being. One of your ancestors."

In Queen Anne's County, Chris Pupke proposed a memorial for the approximately 435 Black soldiers who fought for the Union. "These men, these heroes, fought, bled and died for this country and we are grateful," Pupke said at an April 2025 dedication ceremony at the Kennard African American Cultural Heritage Center. A plaque was unveiled; plans for a statue are underway.

But here's what's missing from mainstream coverage: the Indigenous history. The Nause-Waiwash Band of Indians—descendants of Nanticoke, Choptank, and Pocomoke peoples—have been fighting for recognition and preservation of their history. Chief Donna Wolf Mother Abbott, the first woman chief of a Maryland tribe in known recorded history, talks about how her own family didn't always embrace their Native identity. "There were probably two, three, maybe four generations that were brought up believing that it was disgraceful to claim your heritage, so they didn't teach it to their children," she told a reporter. Her grandmother was one of those who stayed silent.

The tribe hosts an annual festival in September, does educational outreach, fights to protect ancestral sites. In July 2024, a historical sign was unveiled near the Choptank River honoring the Choptank Indian Reservation. But funding is limited, membership small, and much of the history has been lost. "In the next hundred years, the Eastern Shore is probably not even going to be here based on what I see on our trapping grounds," Abbott said, referring to rising sea levels. "I think that's going to be a part of our history that we're going to lose, that we can't get back because a lot of our ancestral land will be gone."

The story of Indigenous people on the Eastern Shore is barely covered. No regular beats exist for it. Few reporters know who to call. The Nause-Waiwash's 32nd annual festival in September 2024 drew maybe a paragraph in the local paper. When it comes to whose history gets preserved, amplified, and funded, Native Americans are still at the back of the line.

### **The Changing Face of the Shore: Immigration and Belonging**

If you'd visited Marydel on August 6, 2025, you would have found a ghost town. Literally. The sidewalks were empty. The stores were closed. The usually bustling taco trucks sat idle. Why? Because ICE had conducted raids, and everyone was terrified.

By evening, 50 people—many of them teenagers—marched through the streets carrying signs: "MELT ICE stop the kidnappings" and "'WORST OF THE WORST?' Rev. Daniel = BEST of the BEST." They walked past mobile home parks where Hispanic families cheered from their porches. They chanted: "Viva la Henderson, viva la Marydel, viva la Templeville, viva la Greensboro."

Ashley Oliver, 24, one of the marchers, explained why she came: "We're just here for those who can't be."

Marydel sits directly on the Maryland-Delaware border. Census data from 2020 shows the Maryland side has a population of 176, of which 115—65%—are Hispanic or Latino. Similar demographics exist in nearby towns: Templeville (70% Hispanic), Henderson (65% Hispanic). These aren't new arrivals, either. Families have been settling here for decades, drawn by agricultural work, poultry processing, construction jobs, and roofing companies.

Yet the Trump administration's immigration crackdown has upended daily life. ICE arrests in Maryland jumped 290% after Trump took office in January 2025, according to White House press releases. While the administration claims to target criminals—"the worst of the worst," in Trump's words—the data tells a different story. By June 2025, of 57,861 people detained by ICE, 41,495 had no criminal convictions.

Pastor Daniel Fuentes Espinal was one of them. He came to Easton from Honduras in 2001 on a six-month tourist visa. He overstayed by 24 years. He married, had three children, became pastor of Iglesia del Nazareno Jesús Te Ama, and served the community without incident. No criminal record. No arrests. Just an immigration violation.

On July 21, 2025, two ICE agents detained him in Easton. He was transferred to Salisbury, then Baltimore, then Louisiana, where he awaited a bond hearing with no scheduled date. His daughter, Clarissa Fuentes Diaz, launched a GoFundMe that quickly raised over $26,000 for legal fees. A rally in Easton drew 150 people.

At an Easton Town Council meeting, every council member commented on the detention. "We are a nation of immigrants, and in my opinion, this administration has turned their backs on the hardworking, honest people like my father that came to this country to make a better life," said Councilmember Maureen Curry, herself the daughter of an immigrant. "Pastor Espinal has been a pillar of our community for nearly 24 years."

Council President Don Abbatiello encouraged residents to contact their congressional representatives: "There is ultimately something wrong with a system where Pastor Espinal and other residents are picked up for what amounts to misdemeanors in civil violations and placed in holding cells far from their loved ones."

Rep. Andy Harris, the Republican congressman representing the Eastern Shore, responded with a statement: "We are trying to determine what leeway the Secretary of Homeland Security has in granting exceptions to deportation orders. That said, the Trump administration has been clear: individuals who are in the country illegally should self-deport and follow the proper, legal process to return."

The economic impact is real. Alfonso Ortiz, who owns Tienda Frontera Variety in Marydel, saw his business drop sharply after the raids. "They were scared for a couple days, and they stopped coming," he said. It rebounded slightly, but foot traffic hasn't returned to normal.

Construction and roofing companies are struggling to fill crews. Farms can't find workers for harvest. The poultry industry—a massive employer on the Eastern Shore—depends heavily on immigrant labor. "What's going to happen to the workforce?" one man asked at a community meeting. "They need the value that immigrants provide."

Victoria Gomez Lozano at the Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center is working overtime distributing "Know Your Rights" guides, red cards that explain what to do when encountering ICE agents, and citizenship prep materials. Her center's emergency fund, normally kept at $10,000 to help with legal fees and emergencies, has been depleted by the surge in arrests.

"A lot of people are calling, very concerned about all the things they see in their social media and all the things they're hearing from the media about the executive orders," said Matthew Peters, the center's executive director. "People are just trying to understand what consequences they may or may not have."

What's missing from coverage: the long history of Hispanic immigration to the Shore, the contributions these families have made, and the systemic reasons they end up in precarious legal situations. The Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center served 3,500 people in 2023—triple what they'd served a decade earlier. That growth reflects both increased immigration and increased need. But local news rarely covers the center unless there's a crisis or a festival. Where are the profiles of families who've been here 20 years? Where are the business owners, the church leaders, the teachers? Where are the stories of second-generation immigrants navigating identity in rural America?

And what about the documented immigrants—those with work visas, green cards, Temporary Protected Status? They're terrified too. The rhetoric from Washington doesn't distinguish. The fear doesn't care about paperwork.

### **LGBTQ+ Rights: Pride, Backlash, and the Battle for Belonging**

The Delmarva Pride Festival draws hundreds to downtown Easton every June. Drag queens perform. Families bring kids for face painting. Vendors sell rainbow merchandise. It's festive, loud, and unapologetically queer in a rural conservative area where many LGBTQ+ people spent years hiding who they are.

And every year, it sparks a fight at the Easton Town Council.

In January 2024, Council President Frank Gunsallus and member David Montgomery voted against approving the festival's public assembly permit. The vote was 3-2 in favor, so the festival proceeded, but Montgomery's comments were revealing: He expressed concerns about "overtly sexualized performances and behavior" at the 2023 event.

By May 2024, Gunsallus escalated. He suggested town funds shouldn't be used for Pride flags or banners. He called the Pride flag "antithetical to America" and said: "I believe that it is a socialist endeavor parading beyond or behind the guise of a social movement." Montgomery doubled down: "I do not believe it is the town's business to make everyone feel good about their identity. This is about the hijacking of the movement by a transexual ideology."

The backlash was swift. Dozens of residents attended the June 3, 2024, council meeting to oppose the statements. Rudy Reyes, a local resident, wrote in the Star Democrat: "Pride is and was meant first as a protest, fighting for the same basic rights that heterosexual people enjoy. This isn't a game."

Mayor Megan Cook distanced herself from Gunsallus and Montgomery: "I feel those comments were dangerous, and they do foster a climate of intolerance and exclusion. Asserting that the Pride flag or support of our LGBTQ community is antithetical to our country or our Constitution is incorrect."

Rev. Elmer Davis, a council member, urged perspective: "We should learn to love everybody. We're not all going to agree on things."

Still, the controversy persisted. Letters to the editor poured in. Mary-Eileen Russell, a local Republican, defended Gunsallus and Montgomery: "There have been five major projects similar to this which we've been successful with. This is not something that focused just on the Eastern Shore. There's a national effort in the country today to begin to look at who buildings and institutions are named after."

The 2025 festival, held June 14, went smoothly. Mayor Cook spoke from the stage, this time bringing her son: "Pride is more than just a festival. It's a powerful reminder of the strength it takes to live authentically and the importance of creating a town where everyone feels safe, valued and supported. I've been proud to stand with you over the years, and I'm especially glad to be here today with my son. It means a lot to me, both as a community leader and as a parent of a son in the LGBTQ+ community."

Tina Jones, co-founder of the Delmarva Pride Center, reflected on the progress: "I think a lot of things have happened. Of course some council members have changed. But also, I think more importantly, conversations have been happening. Look, we don't all agree on every topic. But there are more things that we agree on than we disagree on."

But the tolerance didn't extend everywhere. In Caroline County, the fifth annual Pride festival in Denton proceeded without incident, drawing over 500 attendees. But in smaller, more conservative towns, LGBTQ+ residents still feel isolated. "I don't think that people know that they have a home here," said Jair Devereaux, a festivalgoer from Cambridge. "It's like having a food desert."

The Delmarva Pride Center has been working to expand its reach, establishing chapters in multiple counties to provide year-round support. In February 2025, the board voted to allow local LGBTQ+ organizations to join as chapters, gaining administrative support and resources. DoCo Pride in Dorchester County became the first chapter.

"Basically, the individual chapters will take the lead on programming, events, things that are specific to that geographical area," said Caleb Holland, DoCo Pride chair. "But with the support of the larger Delmarva Pride Center infrastructure."

What's often missing from coverage: the day-to-day realities of being LGBTQ+ in rural areas. The kids who come out in high school and face bullying. The adults who stay closeted at work. The trans people who drive an hour for healthcare. The couples who avoid holding hands in public.

At a January 2025 town hall about LGBTQ+ rights under the Trump administration, panelists discussed what rollbacks of federal protections would mean locally. Fair District Delegate Kris Fair, chair of the Maryland Legislative LGBTQ+ Caucus, warned that Trump's administration would try to cut funding to schools based on sports policies, bathroom access, and the ability to deadname or misgender students. But, he noted, states have legal recourse: "There's been a body of law that's developed that says the United States government cannot, through its funding powers, coerce states to take policy decisions."

Tina Jones, speaking at the town hall as a transgender woman, said: "When you say DEI doesn't matter, it says to other folks, it's fair game on these people now. And it's time to stop it."

### **Hate Crimes and the Rise of Visible Bigotry**

In April 2024, three 13-year-old students at Plum Point Middle School in Calvert County were charged with harassment and hate crimes. According to charging documents, the students displayed swastikas, made Nazi salutes, and directed offensive comments at a Jewish classmate because of their religious beliefs. The behavior continued for months despite repeated requests to stop.

State's Attorney Robert Harvey said: "Maryland was founded on the principle of religious toleration. It is frankly astonishing that nearly 400 years later some people continue to persecute others based upon their religion."

A month later, at the University of Delaware, a student named Jenna Kandeel vandalized a Holocaust memorial display, damaged ten flags, spit on them, and made antisemitic comments including "Fn n n the Jews." She admitted to being "frustrated and triggered" by the memorial. Attorney General Kathy Jennings responded: "The Holocaust is not ancient history. Eighty years later, the world's Jewish population still has not recovered; its survivors are still with us; and I fear that we still have not learned its lessons."

Then came the Zoombombing incident. On May 13, 2024, a Newark City Council meeting was disrupted when two people participating virtually unmuted their microphones and yelled racial slurs, including the n-word. Councilwoman Dwendolyn Creecy, the city's first Black female council member, called it offensive and horrendous. "It's quite funny that you have to do it on a phone line and not here in my face," she said. "But I'm here to take it."

The NAACP demanded swift action. Rev. Blaine Hackett, vice president of the Newark NAACP chapter, called for the city to "forcefully condemn this reprehensible behavior and take all necessary steps to ensure a safe and respectful environment for all participants in public meetings."

In April 2024, St. Daniel's Community Church of Iron Hill, a historic Black church near Newark, Delaware, was vandalized with racial slurs and a broken window. The vandals scrawled the n-word, references to the KKK, and the word "die" all over the exterior walls. Pastor Joseph Field said he believes it was done overnight. "For those of you who don't know it, we have a young man among us who was the victim of police brutality," Carl Snowden said at a Caucus of African American Leaders meeting, pointing to someone in the crowd. "According to what he told me, the police officer told him, 'Nobody can do anything about this. Nothing will be done.' He's about to experience what happens when hornets come together."

These aren't isolated incidents. They reflect a broader trend of emboldened bigotry. But what's striking is how little follow-up coverage these stories get. The arrests happen, the statements are made, and then... silence. Did the middle school students face consequences? Were they referred to juvenile services? What happened to Jenna Kandeel after her arrest? Who were the people who Zoombombed the Newark council meeting?

The gaps in coverage matter because they suggest these are aberrations rather than symptoms of something larger. They make it easier to dismiss each incident as the work of a few bad actors rather than evidence of systemic problems.

## Key Players: The People Who Make This Beat

**Carl Snowden** is impossible to miss. The convener of the Caucus of African American Leaders, a Baltimore-based civil rights activist in his 70s, Snowden has been involved in voting rights cases for more than 30 years. He led the lawsuit against Federalsburg that resulted in the historic voting rights settlement. He's called for renaming the Malkus Bridge. He shows up at council meetings, protests, and community events across the Shore, often wearing a suit and speaking in measured, forceful tones. "The purpose of the Caucus of African American leaders is to make it crystal clear that wherever Black people are, when you go to injure one of us, you're going to have all of us to deal with," he told a crowd in Cambridge. Snowden is both beloved and controversial—some see him as a tireless advocate, others as an outside agitator. But he gets things done. When he brings a lawsuit, he wins. When he calls for accountability, officials listen. He's essential to understanding civil rights organizing on the Shore.

**Victoria Gomez Lozano** arrived in the U.S. from Mexico 16 years ago with her young daughter, eventually settling in Easton. Today, she's deputy director of the Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center and the first Latina appointed to Maryland's Governor's Commission on Hispanic Affairs. She's soft-spoken but determined, the kind of person who quietly keeps an organization running while also navigating high-level policy conversations in Annapolis. When the ICE raids intensified in 2025, Gomez Lozano distributed "Know Your Rights" guides, red cards, and citizenship prep materials. She handles everything from translation services to health insurance enrollment to emergency funds for families facing deportation. "Hopefully we can keep expanding this representation and working for the community," she told a reporter. Her appointment to the governor's commission was significant—representation for the rural Eastern Shore, where the Hispanic population has exploded but political power hasn't caught up.

**Jaelon Terrele Moaney** is a 30-something Mid-Shore native, Talbot County public school graduate, Williams College alum, and now a member of the Maryland Commission on African American History and Culture. He also founded the Needle's Eye Academy, a literacy program inspired by Gil Scott-Heron. Moaney is thoughtful, articulate, and unafraid to challenge conventional narratives. "Our country is really trying to reconcile with wounds that haven't fully healed or even the past few weeks, some that are opening up scars that are deeper than we could imagine," he said after being appointed. He covers Delmarva for the commission, works to preserve Black history through oral histories and archives, and brings a younger voice to conversations dominated by older activists. He's someone to watch—he'll be a major player in how Black history gets told and preserved on the Shore.

**Chief Donna Wolf Mother Abbott** is the first woman chief of a Maryland tribe in known recorded history. At 75, she leads the Nause-Waiwash Band of Indians, descendants of Nanticoke, Choptank, and Pocomoke peoples. She didn't join the tribe until her 30s—her family, like many Indigenous families, had been taught that claiming Native identity was disgraceful. "I knew I was Native American, but it wasn't something that we shouted from the rooftops," she said. Now she fights to preserve history, language, and traditions that were nearly lost. She's blunt about the challenges: "In the next hundred years, the Eastern Shore is probably not even going to be here based on what I see on our trapping grounds." Rising sea levels threaten ancestral lands.
