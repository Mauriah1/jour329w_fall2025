### 1. Understanding the Race Beat on the Eastern Shore  

Covering race and diversity here is nothing like the daily churn of Baltimore or the policy‑lab of DC. The Eastern Shore is eight counties stitched together by a slow‑moving tide, a shared sense of place, and very distinct local cultures. One day you might be in the historic downtown of Easton, listening to a jazz band at a Juneteenth celebration, and the next you’re in a modest community center in Cambridge, watching a council meeting where a bridge is being renamed after a civil‑rights heroine. The beat is a mosaic of celebrations, reckonings, and occasional flashpoints that surface when a policy decision—or an ICE raid—hits a small town.

Covering this beat is very unique, there is limited coverage but it is a topic that is of equal importance. A typical week has two moving parts. The first is the “event calendar”: Black History Month kick‑offs, the annual Delmarva Pride Festival, Juneteenth parades, Hispanic Heritage block parties, and the odd council vote on a DEI statement. Those events demand quick turns, a handful of quotes, and a clear sense of the community’s mood. The second part is the slower, investigative current—voting‑rights settlements in Federalsburg, the ongoing push to rename the Frederick C. Malkus Jr. Bridge in Cambridge, or the $400 million equity investment announced on a Juneteenth in Dorchester. Those stories take weeks of records requests, background research, and building trust with the people who live the history every day.

From 2024‑2025 the threads that keep pulling the beat together are: a) the reclamation of historic memory (Tubman markers, Civil‑War soldier plaques, the “Gloria Richardson Bridge” campaign), b) the push‑and‑pull over DEI statements and diversity training in county councils, and c) the emergence of the Hispanic and LGBTQ communities as visible, organized forces on a largely rural landscape. When you walk the Shore, you’ll find yourself moving between reverence for the past and the urgency of today’s equity battles, often in the same block.

### 2. The Players You’ll Get to Know  

The people who keep the beat alive are not the state legislators who pop in for a photo op; they’re the folks who organize the Juneteenth ceremony at the Academy Art Museum, the activist who leads the Caucus of African American Leaders in Cambridge, and the board members who keep the Pride Festival afloat year after year. You’ll quickly notice a power map that looks less like a hierarchy and more like a web of overlapping circles.

At the center of most civil‑rights actions sits **Carl Snowden** – convener of the Caucus of African American Leaders. ⭐ He’s the one you’ll call when a bridge‑renaming campaign stalls, because he can rally dozens of churches, NAACP chapters, and community groups around a single demand. In Dorchester, the same caucus was the engine behind the “Gloria Richardson Bridge” proposal after the Malkus Bridge controversy.

When you need the Hispanic perspective, you’ll turn to **Victoria Gómez Lozano**. ⭐ As deputy director of the Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center and a member of the Governor’s Commission on Hispanic Affairs, she can point you to the latest immigration‑service data, connect you with families who have just arrived, and explain why a block party in Easton matters beyond the food. She was the first source quoted when the Hispanic Heritage Festival was announced.

The LGBTQ community’s voice comes through **Tina Jones** and **Kyle O’Donnell**, co‑founders of the Delmarva Pride Center. ⭐ Jones chairs the board and is the one who explains why a council vote in Easton that blocked a Pride permit sparked months of heated op‑eds. O’Donnell, as a board member, gives you the historical context of how a rural Pride Festival grew from a single parade in 2020 to a family‑friendly downtown celebration in 2024.

In Dorchester, the younger bridge between state policy and local history is **Jaelon Moaney**. ⭐ As vice chair of the Maryland Commission on African American History and Culture, he is a native Mid‑Shore son who can speak fluently about both the commission’s $5 million grant program and the lived experience of Cambridge residents who marched for civil‑rights in the 1960s.

On the more conservative side of the DEI debate, you’ll hear from **Dave Stepp**, a Talbot County Council member who framed the DEI statement as “politicized.” ⭐ He’s the voice you need when you’re covering the council’s repeated attempts to repeal the statement, and his arguments will often be quoted back‑to‑back with those of **Keasha Haythe**, the only African‑American woman on that same council, who repeatedly defended the statement as essential for inclusion.

Finally, **Lajan Cephas**, the mayor of Cambridge, is the municipal leader who has to balance the city’s “Just Communities” designation, the bridge‑renaming push, and the demand for more affordable housing. Though she isn’t mentioned three times in the stories, she is the go‑to for any question about how Cambridge translates state equity funding into concrete projects.

When a story breaks—say, an ICE detention of a local pastor—your first call should be to the person who already has the community’s trust. In Talbot that’s Gómez Lozano, who can put you in touch with the pastor’s congregation and the local Democratic Forum that organized the rally. In Dorchester, if a new historic marker is being installed, you’ll want the caucus convener Snowden and the local historian Alex Green. The pattern is simple: the person who organized the event is also the person who can give you the backstory, the opposing view, and the next step.

### 3. How Stories Get Written Here  

The strongest Eastern Shore pieces have a clear, almost ritualistic structure. They open with a vivid scene—a crowd gathered on Main Street for a Juneteenth drum circle, a council chamber where the lights flicker as the DEI resolution is read aloud. That opening sets the local color, then the story moves into the “who said what” section, usually quoting the high‑priority source first (Snowden, Gómez Lozano, Jones, or Moaney), followed by the counter‑voice (Stepp or Haythe). The middle of the story weaves in the historical anchor—perhaps the 1909 “Gloria Richardson Bridge” petition or the 1863 U.S. Colored Troops exhibit—tying the present moment to a longer narrative. The closing often ends with a forward‑looking line: a promise of a follow‑up meeting, a scheduled community forum, or a call for residents to attend the next council session.

Key phrases repeat across the beat: “community celebration,” “historic redress,” “DEI statement,” “heritage,” and “justice.” When you see a headline like *“Easton Town Council faces backlash over DEI repeal”* you’ll notice the body leans heavily on council members’ statements, then brings in community activists like the Delmarva Pride Center board to illustrate the human impact. In contrast, a story about the *“Frederick Douglass plaque unveiling”* leans on descendants, museum curators, and city officials, with very little political spin.

Because the Shore’s audience is hyper‑local, the dateline matters. A story that mentions “Talbot County” in the lede signals to readers that the piece is about their backyard; it also tells editors the story belongs in the county‑specific section rather than the general “state” page. The cadence of quotes—activist, then elected official, then an ordinary resident—creates a sense of balance that readers on the Shore expect.

### 4. Building Your Beat  

The first week on the beat is all about planting seeds. Start each Monday by scanning the Easton Economic Development Corporation’s calendar, the Cambridge City Council agenda, and the Delmarva Pride Center’s Facebook updates. Those three feeds alone will give you the next three days of events. Then, fire off introductory emails to the high‑priority sources: let Carl Snowden know you’re the new reporter covering the Caucus, send a quick note to Victoria Gómez Lozano introducing yourself and asking for a coffee, and request a brief meeting with Tina Jones to understand the Pride Center’s annual timeline.

Relationship‑building is less about a single interview and more about showing up consistently. Attend the monthly “Heroes of the Chesapeake” Juneteenth gathering at the Academy Art Museum, sit in on the quarterly Caucus of African American Leaders meeting, and volunteer for a shift at the Hispanic Heritage Block Party. Those moments give you “in‑the‑room” credibility and let you hear the off‑the‑record concerns that later become story angles.

Records requests are another cornerstone. For the Federalsburg voting‑rights settlement, request the court docket, the settlement agreement, and any correspondence between the city attorney and the DOJ. In Dorchester, when the bridge‑renaming campaign resurfaces, pull the original city‑council minutes from the 2020 Malkus Bridge debate and the 2024 “Gloria Richardson Bridge” proposal. Those documents often contain language that reveals the power dynamics you can quote.

Balancing reactive coverage with enterprise work is a juggling act. When the council votes on the DEI statement on a rainy Tuesday, you’ll be writing a deadline piece that night. But the next day, pull the DEI policy draft, interview the training vendor, and start a longer piece on how DEI statements have fared in other rural counties. Over time you’ll learn to carve out two hours each afternoon for deep‑dive research while the mornings stay reserved for the day‑to‑day pulse.

Geography is the hidden editor. Talbot and Dorchester sit on the water and have a larger media footprint; Wicomico and Kent have fewer daily stories but richer historical angles. Keep a mental map: if a story about “Black watermen” breaks in Wicomico, call Dr. Cliff Coppersmith first because his expertise was highlighted in the Cardin round‑table. If a new Hispanic community center opens in Talbot, reach out to Gómez Lozano. By matching the story’s geographic seed to the source most associated with that county, you’ll always have the most authoritative voice.

### 5. County‑by‑County Breakdown  

**Talbot County (Easton, Oxford, St. Michael’s)**  
Talbot feels like the cultural heartbeat of the Shore. Its demographics are a blend of long‑standing African‑American families, a growing Hispanic population, and an increasingly visible LGBTQ community. The calendar over the past two years reads like a festival program: Black History Month kick‑offs at the Academy Art Museum, Juneteenth drum circles, a “Frederick Douglass Unity Day” that raised funds for the local cultural center, and the Delmarva Pride Festival that drew families downtown in June 2024. At the same time, the county council has been wrestling with a DEI statement—first proposed in 2022, then repeatedly repealed and re‑proposed, with council members Dave Stepp pushing a repeal and Keasha Haythe defending it. The tension came to a head on June 9 2024 when Councilmembers Frank Gunsallus and David Montgomery voted against the Pride‑festival permit, sparking a wave of op‑eds by columnist Rudy Reyes and a community backlash that filled the council chambers for weeks.  

The people who anchor Talbot’s stories are the high‑priority sources you’ll hear most often. Carl Snowden (though more active in Dorchester) often shows up at council meetings when civil‑rights language is debated. Victoria Gómez Lozano, now a deputy director at the Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center, coordinates the Hispanic Heritage Block Party each September and can explain why that event matters to a community that still worries about ICE raids. Tina Jones and Kyle O’Donnell of the Delmarva Pride Center keep the LGBTQ narrative moving forward, from the 2024 council vote to the successful Pride Festival in June 2024. When you need a quick quote on the DEI controversy, Dave Stepp’s “politicized DEI” line is always on hand, while Keasha Haythe provides the counter‑balance, emphasizing that the statement “protects our most vulnerable residents.”  

A practical tip for Talbot: whenever you hear about a council meeting, call the Easton Town Council’s clerk immediately—most agendas are posted a week in advance, and the clerk can point you to the public comment period where community members often reveal the next story angle. Also, the Academy Art Museum’s staff are a goldmine for Juneteenth perspectives; they’ll gladly introduce you to volunteers like Tyniece Savoy who can speak to the event’s evolution over the past decade.

**Dorchester County (Cambridge, Church Creek)**  
Dorchester’s narrative is a mix of heritage reclamation and contemporary activism. The county’s African‑American history is front‑and‑center: Alex Green’s discovery of a rare Harriet Tubman photograph sparked a statewide conversation in December 2023, and the Caucus of African American Leaders, led by Carl Snowden, has been the driving force behind renaming the Frederick C. Malkus Jr. Bridge to the “Gloria Richardson Bridge.” The caucus held its first in‑person meeting in early June 2024, with Alpha Kappa Alpha sorority members flooding the Cambridge City Council chambers to demand the change. By May 2024, the bridge‑renaming proposal had become a rallying point for a coalition that also pushed for a historic plaque honoring the city’s Black civil‑rights pioneers.  

The county’s other major thread is the “Just Community” designation announced on Juneteenth 2024, backed by a $400 million state equity investment aimed at correcting historic redlining and mass‑incarceration impacts. The ceremony at Bethel AME Church brought together the Caucus, local NAACP members, and state officials, and it was Jaelon Moaney—our high‑priority vice chair of the Maryland Commission on African American History and Culture—who explained how the grant money would be allocated to affordable housing and small‑business grants.  

When you cover Dorchester, remember that the Caucus of African American Leaders (⭐) is the hub for any civil‑rights‑related story. Reach out to Snowden for background, then ask Alex Green for the human‑interest angle on the Tubman photo. The city council’s meetings are streamed live, and the minutes often include the exact language proposed for the bridge rename, which makes a great pull‑quote. If you need a quick cultural context, the Alpha Kappa Alpha sorority’s members are always eager to discuss their role in the bridge‑renaming campaign.

**Kent County (Chestertown, Chestertown)**  

Kent is the quiet storyteller of the Shore. Its demographic snapshot is small, but the county punches above its weight with a deep sense of place. The biggest recurring events are the Martin Luther King Jr. Breakfast and Banquet each January, and the “Heroes of the Chesapeake” Juneteenth celebration in mid‑June, both anchored at St. Michael’s Community Center and the Garfield Center for the Arts. In January 2024, the Chestertown community and Washington College honored Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. with a breakfast that featured a keynote from Devon Beck, a local activist. Two weeks later, the “Legacy Day” celebration highlighted Black fraternal societies, and in August 2024 the “Authentic Selves” photo exhibit at the Garfield Center gave voice to trans and non‑binary residents.  

The key sources in Kent are less frequent but deeply trusted. When Senator Ben Cardin convened a round‑table with Black watermen in February 2024 at Chesapeake College (though the college sits in Wicomico, the participants were Kent residents), the story leaned heavily on the testimonies of Vera Meredith and Marc Castelli, both community members who have lived the watermen’s legacy for generations. The round‑table’s outcomes—calls for state funding for a museum—still echo in Kent’s local council meetings.  

A good rule of thumb for Kent: the community centers (St. Michael’s, Garfield) have a modest communications staff that can connect you with the event organizers. When a Juneteenth celebration is announced, ask the museum curator for a one‑sentence “why this matters” soundbite; it will often become the lede of your piece. And because Kent’s stories are tightly interwoven with watermen heritage, keep the “Black watermen” phrase in your mental lexicon—it signals relevance to the audience.

**Queen Anne’s County (Centreville)**  

Queen Anne’s is a county where history is still being written in stone. The most visible thread over the past year has been the push for a memorial honoring the U.S. Colored Troops—roughly 435 Black Union soldiers who enlisted from the county during the Civil War. The proposal surfaced in September 2024 when Chris Pupke, past president of the Queen Anne’s County Historical Society and advisory board member at the Kennard African American Heritage Center, announced the plan during a council meeting. By the end of the year, the plaque had been installed at the Kennard Center, drawing veterans, descendants, and schoolchildren for a dedication ceremony.  

Because the county’s racial‑justice conversations are largely centered on this historic commemoration, the primary source you’ll need is Chris Pupke (who appears twice in the coverage). He can provide the research that underpins the plaque’s inscription and the community’s reaction—often a mix of pride and a call for more inclusive curricula in the county schools. Another useful contact is the Kennard African American Heritage Center itself; its staff can introduce you to local educators who have integrated the troops’ story into elementary lessons.  

When you hear a rumor about a new “Colored Troops” project, call the Kennard Center directly. The center’s events calendar is posted on the county’s tourism website and usually lists any upcoming dedication ceremonies, allowing you to be first on the scene.

**Caroline County (Federalsburg, Denton, Ridgely)**  

Caroline’s beat is a blend of legal redress and emerging LGBTQ visibility. In April 2024 the Federalsburg voting‑rights lawsuit reached a settlement: the city issued a formal apology, paid $260 K, and promised restorative actions for the community that had been disenfranchised for decades. The settlement was signed at the historic “Harlem’s Kitchen” restaurant, a local landmark that now serves as a reminder of the county’s civil‑rights legacy. That same year, Denton hosted the fourth annual Caroline Pride Festival in early June, drawing roughly a thousand attendees and marking a shift from a modest parade to a full‑scale family festival with music, food trucks, and a youth stage.  

The high‑priority source for the voting‑rights story is Jaelon Moaney (⭐), who, as a native Mid‑Shore commissioner, explained how the settlement ties into broader state‑wide efforts to correct historic disenfranchisement. For the Pride Festival, the Delmarva Pride Center’s board (including Tina Jones and Kyle O’Donnell) provides the narrative of growth and the challenges of organizing a queer event in a largely rural environment.  

A practical tip: Caroline’s municipal clerks are very responsive when you reference the settlement date—April 7 2024—because it’s a landmark moment for the county. They will often share the original press release, which contains quotes from the city attorney and community leaders you can embed. For Pride, the festival’s organizers keep a public Google Drive of photos and press kits; request access early so you have high‑resolution images for your story.

**Wicomico County (Chesapeake College, Salisbury)**  

Wicomico’s singular race‑related story in the recent window was Senator Ben Cardin’s February 2024 round‑table with Black watermen, their families, and documentary subjects at Chesapeake College. The event was framed as a federal‑level acknowledgment of the watermen’s contribution to the Chesapeake Bay’s cultural heritage. Dr. Cliff Coppersmith, a historian, and community members Vera Meredith and Marc Castelli were the primary voices quoted, providing personal anecdotes about the watermen’s generational knowledge and the need for preservation funding.  

While Wicomico does not have the same volume of events as Talbot or Dorchester, the county’s significance lies in its ability to attract state‑level attention. When you cover a story that references the watermen, start by contacting Dr. Coppersmith—he’s the historian who can explain why the watermen’s narrative matters beyond a single exhibition. Then follow up with the community members to capture the lived experience. The college’s public affairs office will also have a media kit from the round‑table that includes high‑resolution photos and a list of attendees.

**Calvert County (Huntingtown)**  

Calvert’s race coverage is brief but pointed. In August 2024 three middle‑school students from Huntingtown were charged under Maryland’s hate‑crime statutes for alleged anti‑Black conduct. The case sparked a community discussion about how schools address bias and the role of law enforcement in adolescent conflicts. While no high‑priority source is named in the coverage, the school district’s equity coordinator—often a deputy superintendent—will be your go‑to for official statements. The local sheriff’s office can also provide context on how hate‑crime statutes are applied in the county.  

When a hate‑crime story breaks, reach out to the school’s principal first; they can give you the policy response and any upcoming restorative‑justice workshops. Then call the sheriff’s office for the legal perspective. This two‑step approach ensures you capture both the educational and enforcement angles.

**St. Mary’s County (Leonardtown)**  

St. Mary’s entered the race beat through a very different lens: a truth‑and‑reconciliation hearing on historic lynchings held on November 16 2024. The Maryland Lynching Truth & Reconciliation Commission invited descendants of lynching victims Benjamin Hance and Charles Whitley to speak, framing the hearing as a “healing” moment for the county. The event was covered as a solemn public acknowledgment of past violence and an invitation for the community to confront a painful legacy.  

The key source for this story is the commission itself, which provides official transcripts and a list of participants. If you want a personal angle, ask the descendants—if they are willing—to share why speaking out now matters to them. The county historical society also maintains the archival records of the lynchings; they can point you to photographs and newspaper clippings that enrich your background.  

A tip for St. Mary’s: the county’s public library hosts a “History Hour” once a month, often featuring the commission’s staff. Attending those sessions can give you early notice of upcoming hearings or community‑driven memorial projects.

**Charles County (Unnamed town)**  

Charles County’s story is a short, procedural one: a proposal to rename “Black History Month” failed to advance on February 5 2025. The debate highlighted how even symbolic gestures can become flashpoints in a county where the name of the month is tied to broader conversations about representation. While the coverage did not name a specific advocate, the county commissioners’ meeting minutes list the proposal’s sponsor and the opposition’s arguments.  

When you see a name‑change proposal on your radar, the first thing to do is download the council agenda and look for the “public comment” section. That’s where community members often insert personal anecdotes that become the human core of your story. In this case, you could interview a resident who supports the rename and one who opposes it, creating a balanced portrait of the county’s divide.

### 6. Stories Waiting to Be Told  

One of the best ways to stretch the beat is to look for the gaps between the events we already covered. First, the *“Just Community”* designation that was announced on Juneteenth 2024 for Cambridge has yet to be followed up with a concrete inventory of the projects funded. You could start by requesting the county’s equity‑investment plan, then sit down with a community development officer and a local resident who has applied for a grant. That would give you a piece that moves from the celebratory announcement to the lived impact on families trying to repair historic disinvestment.

Second, the *“Hispanic Heritage Festival”* in Easton has become an annual fixture, but there’s no deep dive into the second‑generation Latino youth who are now leading the organizing committee. Interview the youth leaders, ask about their aspirations, and connect their story to the broader trend of a growing Hispanic population on the Shore—something that Victoria Gómez Lozano has been tracking through the CMRC’s service numbers. That angle would shift the coverage from a one‑day event to a longer narrative about community building.

Third, the *“No Kings”* protesters who joined the Pride Festival in June 2024 were mentioned only in passing. Their coalition—bringing together Black activists, labor groups, and LGBTQ allies—offers a rich case study of cross‑movement solidarity in a rural setting. Begin by reaching out to the Delmarva Pride Center’s board, then find the community organizers who coordinated the joint march. Their perspective could illustrate how identity politics evolves on the Shore, especially as the county council debates DEI statements.

A fourth idea centers on the *“ICE detention of Pastor Daniel Fuentes Espinal”* in Talbot. While the immediate coverage focused on the rally and council prayer, there is little reporting on the longer‑term legal battle, the pastor’s congregation’s response, and how immigration enforcement is reshaping faith‑based advocacy in the region. Start by speaking with Gómez Lozano, then request the court docket and interview an immigration lawyer who has represented the pastor. That would turn a breaking‑news piece into a longitudinal investigation.

Finally, the *“Harriet Tubman photo”* discovered by Alex Green sparked a national conversation about representation in historic narratives. Yet the story of how that single photograph traveled from a local tour guide’s collection to a national museum exhibition remains untold. Follow the trail: interview Green, the museum curator, and the descendant families who helped authenticate the image. The result would be a compelling narrative about how local historians shape national memory.

### 7. Themes Across Counties  

Across the eight counties the beat repeatedly circles three big themes. The first is **heritage reclamation**—whether it’s the bridge‑renaming in Cambridge, the U.S. Colored Troops plaque in Queen Anne’s, or the Harriet Tubman photograph in Dorchester. Each of these stories ties a physical marker (a bridge, a plaque, a photo) to a collective desire to rewrite the public landscape. The second is **institutional resistance versus community advocacy**, most visible in the DEI‑statement fight in Talbot and the hate‑crime charges in Calvert. In both cases, a local governing body pushes back against what activists deem essential equity measures, prompting a back‑and‑forth of public comment, op‑eds, and council votes. The third theme is **the emergence of new demographic voices**—the Hispanic community’s block parties, the LGBTQ Pride festivals, and the growing Native‑American presence highlighted by the Nause‑Waiwash Band’s historic road‑sign unveiling in Cambridge.  

You’ll notice that the coverage spikes around June, when Juneteenth, Pride, and many bridge‑renaming ceremonies converge. That month creates a “cultural density” that the press leverages for multiple stories. Conversely, the winter months tend to feature more policy‑oriented pieces—settlements, council votes, and legislative hearings—because the event calendar slows down and the beat leans on the deeper, institutional narratives.  

These patterns tell us that the Eastern Shore’s race beat is both *event‑driven* and *memory‑driven*. The community celebrates its past while simultaneously negotiating how that past informs current policy debates. The stories echo each other across counties: Dorchester’s bridge‑renaming conversation mirrors Talbot’s DEI debate, and the Hispanic Heritage Block Party in Talbot reflects a similar cultural affirmation happening in Caroline’s “Haitian citizenship” session in February 2025. Recognizing those echoes helps you frame a story not as an isolated incident but as part of a broader regional dialogue.

### 8. Coverage Analysis  

Looking at the two‑year archive, Talbot and Dorchester dominate the coverage count—eight and six events respectively—largely because they host the most public festivals and council controversies. Wicomico, Calvert, and St. Mary’s appear only once or twice, mostly because their stories were single‑event pieces (the Cardin round‑table, the hate‑crime charges, and the lynching hearing). This uneven distribution suggests a gap: the smaller counties—Kent, Queen Anne’s, Caroline—receive fewer deep‑dive pieces despite having rich historical material (e.g., the Colored Troops plaque, the voting‑rights settlement).  

Source diversity is similarly skewed. High‑priority voices such as Carl Snowden, Victoria Gómez Lozano, and Dave Stepp appear repeatedly, giving those perspectives a strong platform. However, the coverage underrepresents grassroots voices from the Hispanic and immigrant communities—apart from Gómez Lozano, there are no regular quotes from undocumented residents or from the Haitian community beyond a single citizenship presentation in February 2025. Likewise, the Native‑American perspective is limited to the Nause‑Waiwash Band’s road‑sign unveiling, with no follow‑up on ongoing tribal initiatives.  

Missing angles include: a longitudinal look at how the $400 million equity investment is being allocated across the Shore, an investigation into the outcomes of the Federalsburg voting‑rights settlement after one year, and a comparative study of DEI statements in rural counties versus urban jurisdictions. Those gaps represent opportunities for enterprise reporting that can move the beat beyond event recaps to accountability journalism.

### 9. A Day‑to‑Day: What It May Look Like for You  

A typical week starts on Monday morning with a quick scan of the county council calendars—Talbot, Dorchester, and Queen Anne’s all post their agendas online by 9 a.m. You jot down any public‑comment periods that line up with ongoing DEI debates or bridge‑renaming votes. Then you check the social feeds of the Delmarva Pride Center, the Chesapeake Multicultural Resource Center, and the Cambridge Historical Society for any last‑minute event announcements. By mid‑morning you have a short list of “must‑watch” stories for the next 48 hours.  

Tuesday and Wednesday are often the “reactive” days. If a council votes on the DEI statement on Tuesday, you’re drafting a deadline piece that night, weaving in quotes from Dave Stepp, Keasha Haythe, and a community activist who attended the public comment. Thursday is reserved for deeper work: you might be in the archives of the Kent Historical Society requesting the original enlistment rolls of the U.S. Colored Troops, or you could be filing a public‑records request for the Federalsburg settlement documents. Friday you head out to a community event—perhaps the “Heroes of the Chesapeake” Juneteenth celebration in Kent—recording sound bites and taking photographs for the weekend’s feature.  

When a big breaking story hits—say, an ICE raid in Talbot—your routine flips. You drop everything, call Victoria Gómez Lozano for the immediate community response, then race to the scene to capture the atmosphere and talk to residents. On quieter days you use the time to nurture relationships: meet a coffee with Carl Snowden to discuss the upcoming caucus meeting, or sit down with a high‑school teacher at the Kennard Center to talk about Black History Month curricula. By the end of each week you’ll have a mix of deadline pieces, a longer‑form story in the works, and a handful of new contacts added to your phone.

### 10. Navigating Sensitivities & Interview Tips  

When you’re covering **Talbot**, remember that discussions about immigration enforcement and the Frederick Douglass Unity Day can stir deep trauma. Approach Pastor Daniel Fuentes Espinal’s family and the Hispanic community with gentle phrasing—ask them to share what “safety” looks like for them rather than probing the details of any ICE encounter. Let them set the pace and always offer to review the quote before it goes live.

In **Calvert** and **Sussex**, the hate‑crime charges involving middle‑school students are especially delicate. Those students are minors, so you must obtain parental permission and frame questions around the school’s policies rather than the alleged actions. Emphasize the broader conversation about bias education without singling out the individual children.

When you speak with members of the **Cambridge** Native‑American community—especially the Nause‑Waiwash Band—use the terms the tribe prefers (e.g., “Choptank River Reservation”) and avoid any language that evokes colonial conquest. The band’s chief will often remind you to “talk about preservation, not possession,” so let that guide your line of inquiry.

In **Caroline**, the Pride Festival and the Juneteenth celebration often share the same community space. Ask LGBTQ participants how they navigate a town that simultaneously honors Black history and hosts a queer event—focus on the lived experience of overlap rather than positioning the two as competing.

Finally, in the **Turner Station** neighborhood of Baltimore (the historic Black community referenced in the August 2024 break‑ins), the community’s healing initiatives are deeply personal. When you interview residents like Courtney Speed, start by acknowledging the pain of the break‑ins and then ask what “hope” looks like for them now.
